Script 4: The UFC Let Them Go. Then Everything Changed.
Mike Perry and Francis Ngannou. Source file: pn/vo.txt (the narration that was recorded)
May sixteenth, twenty twenty-six. The Intuit Dome in Los Angeles. Netflix cameras rolling. And Mike Perry — a man the UFC let walk out the door five years ago — is standing in a cage again, covered in blood, arm raised, after stopping Nate Diaz in the second round.
On the same card, just hours earlier, Francis Ngannou — the man Dana White said would never come back — walked into that same cage and put Philipe Lins to sleep in the first round. Nineteen wins. Three losses. Twenty-two consecutive MMA fights without being stopped. At thirty-nine years old.
Two fighters. Same night. Same arena. Same Netflix broadcast. Both former UFC athletes the promotion chose not to keep. And both of them just delivered two of the most electric performances of the year.
This isn't a coincidence. This is a pattern. And it raises a question that Dana White really doesn't want anyone asking. What happens when the fighters the UFC throws away turn out to be more valuable on the outside?
To understand how we got here, you have to understand where these two came from. And honestly, their stories couldn't be more different.
Mike Perry grew up in Flint, Michigan. Not exactly a city known for giving kids a smooth ride into life. Perry has talked openly about a rough childhood — drugs, house arrest, probation violations, jail. He started boxing at eleven years old, and fighting became the one thing that gave his life structure. By the time he turned professional in MMA, he was a walking highlight reel. Nine straight knockouts to start his career. Nine. Every single opponent put away before the final bell.
The UFC signed him, and for a while, it looked like Platinum Mike Perry was going to be a problem for the entire welterweight division. His Octagon debut? First-round knockout. His second fight? Another knockout. And then came the Jake Ellenberger fight.
That elbow is still one of the most violent knockouts in UFC history. It was an immediate Knockout of the Year contender. Perry earned Performance of the Night. The hype was real.
But here's the thing about Mike Perry. For every moment of brilliance, there was a moment of chaos. He'd knock out Jake Ellenberger one fight and then lose a decision the next. He'd look like a future contender and then get finished by Geoff Neal — his first career knockout loss. Over his last fifteen UFC fights, Perry went seven and eight. Not terrible. But not the trajectory of a guy the UFC was going to build around.
His contract expired in twenty twenty-one. The UFC didn't re-sign him. And honestly? Most people forgot about him.
But Perry didn't forget about himself. He signed with Bare Knuckle Fighting Championship in October twenty twenty-one, and something clicked. Maybe it was the rawness of bare-knuckle that fit his personality. Maybe it was the freedom of being his own brand. But Perry went on an absolute tear.
He beat Julian Lane. He beat Michael \"Venom\" Page by majority decision. Then he knocked out Luke Rockhold — a former UFC middleweight champion — in two rounds. Then Eddie Alvarez — a former UFC lightweight champion — knocked out in two rounds. That win made Perry the first ever BKFC King of Violence champion. Then Thiago Alves, first-round TKO. Five and oh in bare knuckle. Five wins against legitimate names. Perry wasn't just surviving outside the UFC. He was thriving.
Now, did everything go perfectly? No. This is Mike Perry we're talking about. He took a boxing match against Jake Paul in July twenty twenty-four and got knocked out. But even that loss kept his name in the headlines. Perry was more famous outside the UFC than he ever was inside it.
He came back and beat Jeremy Stephens at BKFC eighty-two. And then the call came — MVP MMA wanted him for their debut card on Netflix. Perry versus Nate Diaz. And Perry delivered exactly what everyone hoped for. A blood-soaked, violent, incredible fight that ended with a second-round corner stoppage. Perry's MMA record moved to fifteen and eight. And suddenly, everyone was talking about a UFC return.
Perry has said he's in talks with the UFC. He's mentioned the White House card. He's called out CM Punk. He's called out Conor McGregor. He's even said he'd take a pay cut to come back. Think about that for a second. A fighter who left the UFC, became a star without them, and is now willing to take less money just to fight in the Octagon again. That tells you something about the pull the UFC still has. But it also tells you something about what Perry built on the outside — enough leverage that the conversation is even happening.
Now let's talk about the other side of this story. Because if Mike Perry's journey is a tale of a brawler finding his lane, Francis Ngannou's journey is something else entirely. It's one of the most remarkable stories in the history of combat sports.
Francis Ngannou was born in Batié, a small village in western Cameroon. His parents divorced when he was six. He was shuffled between relatives who could barely feed their own children. By the time he was ten years old, he was working in sand mines, shoveling sand into trucks for less than two dollars a day. Not training. Not going to school. Shoveling sand. As a child.
Ngannou has said in interviews that he hated his life growing up. He hated the sand mine. He hated everything about where he was. But he had one dream — to become a professional boxer. At twenty-six, with no formal training in any combat sport, he decided to leave Cameroon for Europe. He tried to cross from Africa six different times and failed. He was jailed for two months in Spain for illegally entering Europe by sea. When he finally made it to Paris, he was homeless, sleeping in parking garages, scavenging for food.
And from there — from literally nothing — he walked into an MMA gym, started training, and within a few years was knocking out every single person put in front of him. The UFC signed him. And then he became the most terrifying heavyweight the sport has ever seen.
To understand just how scary this man was — Jairzinho Rozenstruik, one of the hardest hitters in the division. Ngannou finished him in twenty seconds. Cain Velasquez, one of the greatest heavyweights ever. Twenty-six seconds. These weren't decisions. These weren't third-round stoppages. These were blink-and-you-miss-it executions.
And then came Stipe Miocic. The greatest UFC heavyweight champion of all time by the numbers. Ngannou knocked him out fifty-two seconds into the second round at UFC two-sixty. Francis Ngannou became the UFC Heavyweight Champion. The first African-born heavyweight champion in UFC history. A kid from the sand mines of Cameroon holding the most prestigious belt in combat sports.
Then came UFC two-seventy. His title defense against Ciryl Gane. What most people didn't know at the time was that Ngannou fought that entire fight on a torn ACL and MCL. A torn knee. And he still won by unanimous decision, wrestling Gane for five rounds on one leg. It might be the gutsiest performance in heavyweight title fight history.
But behind the scenes, the relationship between Ngannou and the UFC was falling apart. And this is where the story gets really interesting. Because what happened next wasn't about fighting. It was about business.
Ngannou wanted three things the UFC wouldn't give him. He wanted the right to have personal sponsors. He wanted the freedom to box. And he wanted proper health insurance — because the UFC doesn't pay for fighters' training, medical expenses, or development. Ngannou also wanted a shorter contract — three fights — instead of being locked in long-term.
Dana White said publicly that he offered Ngannou the biggest contract in UFC heavyweight history. But Ngannou turned it down. It wasn't about the money. It was about control.
The UFC stripped him of his title. They released him in January twenty twenty-three. And according to multiple reports, they even tried to erase him — editing his name out of footage, scrubbing references to him. Dana White said Ngannou would never return to the UFC.
But Ngannou didn't disappear. He did what no MMA fighter had ever done before. He signed a deal with the PFL that included equity ownership, a leadership role on the Global Advisory Board, the position of Chairman of PFL Africa, a share of event profits, his own personal sponsors, the freedom to box, and — this is the part that matters most — a guaranteed minimum salary of two million dollars for whoever he fought. Francis Ngannou didn't just negotiate a better deal for himself. He negotiated a better deal for his opponents. That's unprecedented in combat sports.
Then he went and boxed Tyson Fury. The lineal heavyweight boxing champion. In Saudi Arabia. And Ngannou — a man with essentially zero professional boxing experience — knocked Fury down in the third round and lost by split decision. The boxing world was in shock. An MMA fighter had nearly beaten the best heavyweight boxer on the planet.
He lost to Anthony Joshua by second-round knockout in his next boxing match. But even in defeat, Ngannou was proving his point — he could compete at the highest level in multiple sports, on his own terms, making more money than the UFC ever offered him.
And then, in April twenty twenty-four, tragedy struck. Ngannou's fifteen-month-old son, Kobe, passed away. It was devastating. Ngannou stepped away from everything. The sports world mourned with him. For a while, it seemed like fighting might be the last thing on his mind.
But Ngannou came back. In October twenty twenty-four, he returned to MMA and knocked out Renan Ferreira in the first round to win the PFL Super Fights Heavyweight Championship. And then the PFL deal fell apart — he fought only once for the promotion in three years, and they released him in early twenty twenty-six. The experiment was over. But by then, Ngannou didn't need them either.
He showed up at MVP MMA one. Same card as Mike Perry. Same Netflix cameras. First-round knockout of Philipe Lins. At thirty-nine years old, Francis Ngannou proved that he is still one of the most dangerous human beings on the planet. And he did it entirely outside the UFC system.
Now here's where these two stories converge, and where it gets really fascinating.
Mike Perry and Francis Ngannou both left the UFC. Both became bigger stars on the outside. Both ended up on the same Netflix card in May twenty twenty-six. But their paths from here couldn't be more different.
Perry wants back in. He's actively pursuing a UFC return. He's willing to take a pay cut. For Perry, the UFC is still the ultimate destination — the place where legacies are made, where the biggest fights happen, where being a UFC fighter still means something.
Ngannou wants nothing to do with it. He's calling out Jon Jones. He's calling out Jake Paul. He's building his foundation in Cameroon, opening MMA gyms, investing in African combat sports infrastructure. For Ngannou, the UFC isn't the destination anymore. It's just one stop on a much bigger journey.
And this split — Perry going back, Ngannou staying independent — perfectly captures the tension at the heart of MMA right now.
The UFC — now operated under TKO Group Holdings, the publicly traded company that also owns WWE — settled a three-hundred-and-seventy-five-million-dollar antitrust lawsuit in twenty twenty-five after a decade of legal battles. Over a thousand fighters accused the promotion of monopolistic practices and suppressing fighter pay. Court documents revealed that TKO's UFC division pulled in one-point-four billion dollars in revenue in twenty twenty-four — and fighters received roughly fourteen and a half percent of it. Compare that to roughly fifty percent in the NBA, NFL, and MLB.
New antitrust lawsuits have already been filed. The fighter pay conversation isn't going away. And every time a fighter like Ngannou leaves the UFC and builds something bigger on his own, it chips away at the UFC's argument that fighters need them more than they need the fighters.
But then you look at Mike Perry — a guy who became a star outside the UFC and still wants to come back — and you realize it's not that simple. The UFC brand still carries weight. The Octagon still means something. And for a lot of fighters, the dream isn't independence. The dream is the UFC.
So who's right? Perry, chasing the Octagon? Or Ngannou, building an empire outside of it?
Maybe the answer is that they're both right. Maybe the answer is that the UFC is still the biggest stage in combat sports, but it's no longer the only stage. And for the first time in the sport's history, fighters have real options. Real leverage. Real proof that life after the UFC isn't just possible — it can be better than anything the UFC ever offered.
Mike Perry is thirty-four. Francis Ngannou is thirty-nine. Both of them are in the final chapters of their fighting careers. But the doors they've kicked open? Those are going to stay open for the next generation.
