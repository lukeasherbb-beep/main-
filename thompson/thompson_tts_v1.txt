This is the story of how Stephen Wonderboy Thompson, a forty-three-year-old karate fighter who had lost five of his last six fights, walked into Madison Square Garden for the final fight of his career and left with a spinning wheel kick knockout. It is also the story of how a fifteen-year-old kid from Simpsonville, South Carolina earned the nickname that followed him for twenty-eight years.

That kid stepped into a kickboxing ring in nineteen ninety-eight to face a twenty-six-year-old opponent with a record of twenty wins and no losses, and nobody in the building gave the teenager a chance. Thompson won decisively, and by the end of the night the ring announcer had given him a name that would stay with him for the rest of his life.

Twenty-eight years later, on November fourteenth, two thousand and twenty-six, Wonderboy fought for the last time at UFC three thirty-four, and he finished it with the same kind of kick that made him famous.

Stephen Randall Thompson was born on February eleventh, nineteen eighty-three, into a family where martial arts came with the house. His father, Ray Thompson, a former professional kickboxer, runs Upstate Karate in Simpsonville, and Ray also served as his son's trainer and manager. Fighting ran through the whole family tree, with his sister Lindsay marrying Carlos Machado, the jiu-jitsu coach who later trained Stephen, and his brother Tony marrying the sister of former UFC middleweight champion Chris Weidman.

Years of training at his father's school gave Stephen a style nobody his age could solve, built on distance, speed, and kicks from angles most opponents never saw coming. That's how a fifteen-year-old ended up in a ring with a grown man, and that's where the Wonderboy story started.

What followed was one of the cleanest records in combat sports, with fifty-seven kickboxing wins and zero losses across thirty-seven amateur bouts and twenty professional fights. Along the way he collected sixteen amateur titles, including a world championship in two thousand and five. By two thousand and six he sat at number one in the World Combat League, a kickboxing promotion founded by Chuck Norris, and yes, that really was the same Chuck Norris.

Nobody could beat him standing up, so Thompson carried that karate into mixed martial arts, where he won his first five professional fights and earned a contract with the UFC.

His octagon debut came on February fourth, two thousand and twelve, at UFC one forty-three, and he knocked his opponent out. His second UFC fight ended in a decision loss, and what came after that loss is where his career really took off.

Thompson strung together seven straight UFC wins, and the names on that list explain why the whole welterweight division started paying attention.

Robert Whittaker, who later became the UFC middleweight champion, didn't make it out of the first round against him in two thousand and fourteen. At the Ultimate Fighter twenty-one Finale in July of two thousand and fifteen, Jake Ellenberger dropped Thompson with a right hand, and Thompson answered with a spinning wheel kick that put Ellenberger down, then landed another one to end the fight in the first round.

That kick against Ellenberger would come back eleven years later, in the final round of his career.

Former welterweight champion Johny Hendricks went next, stopped in the first round in February of two thousand and sixteen, and four months later Thompson won a unanimous decision over Rory MacDonald.

The streak made Thompson the number one contender at welterweight, and on November twelfth, two thousand and sixteen, he walked into Madison Square Garden to fight Tyron Woodley for the UFC title. UFC two oh five was the night Conor McGregor knocked out Eddie Alvarez to become a two-division champion, but the welterweight title fight on that same card turned into a classic of its own.

For long stretches Thompson picked Woodley apart from the outside, and then the fourth round nearly ended everything. Woodley dropped him with right hands, then locked a guillotine choke around his neck and held it for the better part of a minute, and somehow Thompson refused to tap, fought his way loose, and finished the round on top.

After twenty-five minutes, the judges scored it forty-seven to forty-seven, forty-seven to forty-seven, and forty-eight to forty-seven for Woodley, which made it a majority draw and meant Woodley kept the belt.

The rematch came at UFC two oh nine in March of two thousand and seventeen, and this time the judges gave Woodley a majority decision. Twice Thompson had come within a few scorecard points of the title, and twice he went home without it.

Plenty of fighters never recover from a run like that, but Thompson came back in November of two thousand and seventeen and beat Jorge Masvidal at UFC two seventeen, years before Masvidal became one of the biggest stars in the sport.

The years that followed were harder on him. Darren Till beat him on the scorecards, and in two thousand and nineteen Anthony Pettis became the first man to knock him out. There were still big nights, including a five-round win over Geoff Neal in December of two thousand and twenty, but losses to Gilbert Burns and Belal Muhammad pushed him further from the title. In December of two thousand and twenty-two he gave Kevin Holland a beating that ended when Holland's corner stopped the fight after four rounds, and that would be the last win of his career for almost four years.

Shavkat Rakhmonov handed him the first submission loss of his career with a rear-naked choke at UFC two ninety-six in December of two thousand and twenty-three. Joaquin Buckley knocked him out at UFC three oh seven in October of two thousand and twenty-four. In July of two thousand and twenty-five, at UFC Nashville, he fought Gabriel Bonfim through a shin injury he later described as split to the bone, and he lost a split decision that a lot of fans believed he had won.

Heading into November, Thompson had won one of his last six fights at forty-three years old, and the question around him shifted from when he would get another title shot to when he would walk away.

The answer came from home.

Thompson had become a father to a baby girl, who was about five months old by fight night, and he started calling himself Wonderdad. He told Ariel Helwani that fatherhood changed how he looked at his career, that he didn't want to take serious damage at forty-three against much younger fighters, and that he wanted more time at home with his family. His view of the belt had changed too, and he put it plainly to Helwani, saying that he doesn't see the title anymore.

He announced that UFC three thirty-four at Madison Square Garden would be his last fight, in the same building where he had fought Woodley ten years earlier.

The UFC matched him with Charles Radtke, who came in at twelve and five with only two UFC losses, both to ranked opponents, and one of those losses had come against Joaquin Buckley, the same man who knocked Thompson out two years earlier.

The first round played out the way his critics expected, with Radtke taking Thompson down in less than a minute and controlling him with the kind of wrestling that had given Thompson trouble before. Thompson got back up and won plenty of the exchanges on the feet, but Radtke had done enough to take the round.

Round two started with another Radtke takedown against the fence, and that's when the fight began to turn. Thompson got back to his feet and buried a kick into Radtke's body that clearly hurt him, and Radtke had to shoot for another takedown just to make it out of the round, which Thompson stuffed.

Radtke kept looking for takedowns in the third round, and Thompson kept defending them. At two minutes and fifty-four seconds of the round, the spinning wheel kick landed flush, the same weapon that had finished Jake Ellenberger eleven years earlier, and referee Marc Goddard stepped in to stop the fight.

Radtke spent four minutes and forty-seven seconds in control over those three rounds, but Thompson out-landed him sixty-seven to eleven in significant strikes, and in the end the karate won.

Thompson had told himself he wasn't going to cry, and once the fight was over he laid his gloves down in the center of the octagon, the traditional way a fighter says goodbye to the sport.

On the microphone, he thanked the UFC and the fans who had followed him for fourteen years.

Joe Rogan was in tears on the broadcast, and Thompson said afterward that he had never seen Rogan cry in his life. He also revealed that he had predicted the finish before the fight, and Forbes went on to call it the greatest retirement fight victory in UFC history.

Part of the reason that night hit so many people is who Thompson was outside the cage. In a sport built on trash talk, he became known as the kindest fighter in the game. Ariel Helwani even presented him with a custom NMF belt in two thousand and nineteen, a nod to his reputation as the nicest man in mixed martial arts. Even he had limits, and after the Bonfim decision he said he was stepping away from the nice-guy label and called for better education for judges.

Between fights he was back at Upstate Karate, running the children's karate program at his father's school in Simpsonville.

He leaves the sport with a professional MMA record of eighteen wins, nine losses, and one draw, with thirteen of those wins inside the UFC, on top of a fifty-seven and oh kickboxing record that nobody ever managed to blemish.

The numbers only tell part of it. This is a kid from Simpsonville who grew up inside his father's karate school, went fifty-seven and oh as a kickboxer, and came within a draw of a UFC title. He kept going through the losses, became a father, and then walked into Madison Square Garden one last time to finish his career with the kick that defined it.

Stephen Wonderboy Thompson proved you don't need to be a villain to be remembered, and for fourteen years in the UFC, being himself was enough. Thank you for everything, Wonderboy.
