This is the story of Stephen Wonderboy Thompson, a forty-three-year-old karate fighter who had lost five of his last six fights, and the spinning wheel kick he landed at Madison Square Garden in the final fight of his career. It's also the story of a nickname, one that a fifteen-year-old from Simpsonville, South Carolina picked up in a kickboxing ring and carried for twenty-eight years.

In nineteen ninety-eight, that kid stepped into the ring across from a twenty-six-year-old who was twenty and oh. Nobody gave him a chance. Thompson won decisively, and before the night was over the ring announcer had handed him the name he'd keep for the rest of his life.

Twenty-eight years later, on November fourteenth, two thousand and twenty-six, Wonderboy walked out for UFC three thirty-four knowing it was the last time. He went out the way he came in. With his feet.

Stephen Randall Thompson was born on February eleventh, nineteen eighty-three, into a house where fighting was basically the family trade. His father, Ray Thompson, is a former professional kickboxer who runs Upstate Karate in Simpsonville, and Ray also worked as his son's trainer and manager. The family tree only gets more fight-heavy from there. His sister Lindsay married Carlos Machado, the jiu-jitsu coach who later trained Stephen, while his brother Tony married the sister of Chris Weidman, the former UFC middleweight champion.

All those hours in his dad's school produced a style nobody his age could figure out, built on distance and speed, with kicks coming from angles opponents never saw until it was too late. That's how a fifteen-year-old ended up in a ring with a grown man. That's where Wonderboy starts.

From there the record got ridiculous. Fifty-seven kickboxing fights, fifty-seven wins, zero losses, split between thirty-seven amateur bouts and twenty as a pro. Sixteen amateur titles came with it, including a world championship in two thousand and five, and by two thousand and six he was ranked number one in the World Combat League, a kickboxing promotion founded by none other than Chuck Norris. His knockout total changes depending on which source you trust. The zero in the loss column never does.

There was nobody left to beat standing up. Thompson took the karate into mixed martial arts, won his first five pro fights, and got the call from the UFC.

February fourth, two thousand and twelve, UFC one forty-three. His octagon debut ended in a knockout. The second UFC fight didn't go his way, a decision loss, and honestly that loss is where the real career begins.

Seven straight wins followed. Look at the names on that list and you'll see why the whole welterweight division started paying attention.

Robert Whittaker, a future middleweight champion, didn't survive the first round with him in two thousand and fourteen. July of two thousand and fifteen brought the Ultimate Fighter twenty-one Finale, and Jake Ellenberger, who actually dropped Thompson first with a right hand. Thompson got up, spun, and put Ellenberger down with a wheel kick, and when Ellenberger climbed back to his feet, a second one ended it in round one.

Remember that kick. It comes back eleven years later, in the final round of his career.

Johny Hendricks, a former welterweight champ, got stopped in the first round in February of two thousand and sixteen. Four months on, Thompson took a unanimous decision off Rory MacDonald.

Seven in a row made him the number one contender, and on November twelfth, two thousand and sixteen, Thompson walked into Madison Square Garden to fight Tyron Woodley for the welterweight title. Most people remember UFC two oh five as the night Conor McGregor knocked out Eddie Alvarez to become a two-division champion. The title fight further down that card deserves to be remembered just as much.

For long stretches Thompson picked at Woodley from the outside. The fourth round nearly ended everything. Woodley dropped him with right hands, wrapped up a guillotine, and squeezed for the better part of a minute, and somehow Thompson refused to tap, fought his way loose, and finished the round on top.

Twenty-five minutes, three judges, and the cards came back forty-seven to forty-seven, forty-seven to forty-seven, and forty-eight to forty-seven for Woodley. A majority draw. Woodley kept the belt.

They ran it back at UFC two oh nine in March of two thousand and seventeen, and this time the judges gave Woodley a majority decision. Two title fights. A few scorecard points away both times, with nothing to show for it.

That kind of thing breaks fighters. It didn't break him. In November of two thousand and seventeen he beat Jorge Masvidal at UFC two seventeen, years before Masvidal turned into one of the biggest stars in the sport.

The next stretch was rougher. Darren Till beat him on the scorecards, and in two thousand and nineteen Anthony Pettis became the first man to knock him out. There were still good nights, like a five-round win over Geoff Neal in December of two thousand and twenty, but Gilbert Burns and Belal Muhammad both beat him and the title drifted further away. In December of two thousand and twenty-two he put a beating on Kevin Holland until Holland's corner stopped it after four rounds. Nobody knew it at the time. That would be his last win for almost four years.

The losses stacked up from there. Shavkat Rakhmonov submitted him with a rear-naked choke at UFC two ninety-six in December of two thousand and twenty-three, the first submission loss of his career. Ten months later, Joaquin Buckley knocked him out at UFC three oh seven. In July of two thousand and twenty-five, at UFC Nashville, he fought Gabriel Bonfim on a shin he later described as split to the bone, and lost a split decision that a lot of fans thought he'd won.

That was the picture heading into November. Forty-three years old. One win in his last six. Nobody was talking about title shots anymore, just about when he'd walk away.

The answer came from home.

Thompson had become a dad to a baby girl, about five months old by fight night, and he started calling himself Wonderdad. He told Ariel Helwani that fatherhood changed how he looked at his career. He didn't want to take serious damage at forty-three against much younger fighters, and he wanted more time at home with his family. About the belt, he was blunt, telling Helwani he doesn't see the title anymore.

He picked his exit himself. UFC three thirty-four, Madison Square Garden, the same building where he'd fought Woodley ten years earlier.

The UFC gave him Charles Radtke, twelve and five, with just two losses in the UFC, both to ranked opponents. One of them was Joaquin Buckley, the same man who'd knocked Thompson out two years before.

Round one went the way the doubters said it would. Radtke got him down in under a minute and controlled him with the kind of wrestling that had given Thompson trouble before. Thompson scrambled back up and won plenty of the exchanges on the feet, but on the scorecards it didn't matter, because Radtke had done enough to bank the round.

Round two opened with another takedown, Radtke pinning him against the fence. That's when it turned. Thompson got back to his feet and dug a kick into Radtke's body that clearly hurt him, and Radtke had to shoot for another takedown just to survive the round. Thompson sprawled on it.

Radtke kept hunting takedowns in the third, and Thompson kept stuffing them. Two minutes and fifty-four seconds into the round, the wheel kick landed flush, the same weapon that finished Ellenberger eleven years earlier, and referee Marc Goddard stepped in to wave it off. Some outlets are calling it a spin hook kick and others a wheel kick. The name matters a lot less than where it landed.

The stats from that fight are strange. Radtke had four minutes and forty-seven seconds of control time, and Thompson still out-landed him sixty-seven to eleven in significant strikes. The karate won.

Thompson had told himself he wasn't going to cry. Once it was over, he laid his gloves down in the center of the octagon, the traditional way a fighter tells the sport he's done.

On the mic, he thanked the UFC and the fans who'd followed him for fourteen years.

Joe Rogan was crying on the broadcast, and Thompson said later he'd never seen Rogan cry in his life. He'd also predicted the finish before the fight. Forbes went further and called it the greatest retirement fight victory in UFC history.

That night hit people the way it did because of who Thompson was outside the cage. In a sport that runs on trash talk, he was known as the kindest guy in the building, and in two thousand and nineteen Ariel Helwani gave him a custom NMF belt, a nod to his reputation as the nicest man in mixed martial arts. Even the nice guy had a limit. After the Bonfim decision, he said he was stepping away from the nice-guy label and called for better education for judges.

Between fights, he was back home at Upstate Karate, running the kids' program at his dad's school.

He walks away with a professional record of eighteen wins, nine losses, and one draw, thirteen of those wins in the UFC, plus a fifty-seven and oh kickboxing record that nobody ever touched.

Numbers only cover part of it. A kid from Simpsonville grew up inside his father's karate school, went fifty-seven and oh, and came within a draw of a UFC title. He kept going through the losses. He became a father. On his last night at Madison Square Garden, he finished his career with the kick that made him.

You don't have to be the villain to be remembered. Stephen Wonderboy Thompson spent fourteen years in the UFC proving it, just by being himself. Thank you for everything, Wonderboy.
