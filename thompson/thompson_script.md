In 1998, a fifteen-year-old kid from Simpsonville, South Carolina walked into a kickboxing ring to fight a twenty-six-year-old man with a record of twenty and zero. The kid had no business being in there. Everyone in the building knew it. And then he dominated every single round. After the fight, someone asked the older fighter what happened. He said… I wonder why he even stepped in the ring with this boy. The announcer grabbed the mic and said… ladies and gentlemen… Wonderboy.
[FE CARD: "WONDERBOY" — golden text, Thompson fighting stance silhouette]
That nickname stuck for the next twenty-eight years. And last night, at Madison Square Garden, Stephen Wonderboy Thompson made sure it was the last thing anyone would ever call him inside that octagon. Because he left it the only way he knew how.
With a spinning wheel kick to the head.

This is the story of the nicest man to ever throw a punch in the UFC. And how he walked away on his own terms when everyone said he couldn't.
Stephen Randall Thompson was born February eleventh, nineteen eighty-three. His father, Ray Thompson, opened a martial arts school called Upstate Karate that same year. For the Thompson family, training wasn't optional. It was what you did after school the way other kids did homework. Stephen's sister Lindsey fought before he did. His brother's family married into MMA royalty — his connection to former UFC middleweight champion Chris Weidman. Fighting was the family business.
[CLIP: Old photo/footage of young Thompson training at Upstate Karate]
But Stephen didn't just train. He was different. By the time he was a teenager, his karate was so sharp, so fast, so unorthodox, that nobody his age could touch him. That's how he ended up fighting that twenty-six-year-old at fifteen. That's how the Wonderboy legend started.
And then the legend grew.
[FE CARD: "57-0" — large gold numbers with "COMBINED KICKBOXING RECORD" underneath]
Fifty-seven wins. Zero losses. Thirty-seven amateur victories, twenty professional, over forty knockouts, sixteen amateur titles, and a World Amateur Championship gold medal in two thousand and five. He was ranked number one in Chuck Norris's World Combat League. Not a typo. Chuck Norris had a fighting league, and Wonderboy was the best fighter in it.
He was untouchable on his feet. The problem was… kickboxing was dying. The spotlight was moving to MMA. And in two thousand and ten, after an injury in his final kickboxing bout, Thompson made the jump.
[CLIP: Thompson's early MMA fight footage]
He debuted in MMA, went five and oh, and the UFC came calling. On February fourth, two thousand and twelve, Stephen Thompson made his octagon debut at UFC one forty-three. He got a knockout. His second fight was a decision loss. And then something clicked.
Seven straight wins in the UFC. Not just wins — statements.
[FE CARD: "THE RUN" — gold timeline graphic]
Robert Whittaker, who would go on to become the middleweight champion — Thompson stopped him in round one. Jake Ellenberger — spinning wheel kick knockout. The same weapon he'd use in his very last fight, fourteen years later. Johny Hendricks, the former welterweight champion — first round TKO. Rory MacDonald, one of the most dangerous welterweights alive — unanimous decision.
[CLIP: Thompson KO on Ellenberger — spinning wheel kick — LET IT PLAY 4 seconds]
By two thousand and sixteen, Wonderboy wasn't just a contender. He was THE contender. The number one ranked welterweight in the world. And on November twelfth, two thousand and sixteen, he walked into this same building — Madison Square Garden — for the biggest fight of his life.
UFC two oh five. The same card where Conor McGregor knocked out Eddie Alvarez to become the double champ. But buried under that headline was one of the greatest title fights in UFC history.
[CLIP: Thompson vs Woodley I footage]
Thompson versus Tyron Woodley for the welterweight championship. Five rounds of chaos. Thompson picking Woodley apart on the feet. Woodley dropping Thompson twice in the fourth round. Thompson locking up a guillotine choke off the mat. Back and forth, back and forth, for twenty-five minutes in front of a sold-out Garden.
The result? A majority draw. Forty-seven forty-seven, forty-seven forty-seven, forty-eight forty-seven Woodley. No winner. They had to do it again.
[FE CARD: "MAJORITY DRAW" — split screen graphic, Thompson vs Woodley]
The rematch came at UFC two oh nine in March of two thousand and seventeen. This time, Woodley figured Thompson out. Kept him at range, neutralized the karate, and took a majority decision. Wonderboy had come as close to the title as you can get… twice… and walked away with nothing.
That's where a lot of fighters break. When you give everything, when you're RIGHT THERE, and it slips through your fingers — not once but twice — most people aren't the same after that.
But Thompson? He went out and beat Jorge Masvidal. Before Masvidal was Masvidal. He kept fighting. He kept showing up. He kept being Wonderboy.

And then the losses started coming. Darren Till outpointed him. Anthony Pettis knocked him out for the first time in his career — a moment that shook the entire MMA world. Gilbert Burns. Belal Muhammad. Shavkat Rakhmonov submitted him. Joaquin Buckley knocked him out cold.
[FE CARD: "1-5 IN HIS LAST 6" — dark background, somber tone]
One win in his last six fights heading into last night. The skills were still there in flashes, but at forty-three years old, the game had passed him by. That's what people said. The conversation shifted from when will Wonderboy get another title shot to when will Wonderboy retire.
And here's the thing about Stephen Thompson — he heard all of it. He knew.
But something else happened that changed everything.
[CLIP: Thompson with his baby daughter — social media photo/video]
About five months ago, Stephen Thompson became a father. A baby girl. He started calling himself Wonderdad. And he told Ariel Helwani something that everyone who's ever become a parent understands.
He said his daughter changed everything. He said he wanted his body nice and healthy five, ten, fifteen years from now so they could go hiking and riding bikes together. He said he didn't see the title anymore. He said his body didn't recover like it used to.
[FE CARD: "WONDERDAD" — warm gold tones, soft lighting feel]
He told the world he was done. November fourteenth, two thousand and twenty-six. Madison Square Garden. UFC three thirty-four. One last fight.
And the UFC gave him Charles Radtke. Twelve and five, riding a two-fight win streak, tough as they come. This wasn't a layup. This was a real fight against a real opponent who had every reason to spoil the party.
[CLIP: Radtke highlight or weigh-in face-off]
The first round went exactly how everyone feared. Radtke pressed forward, shot a takedown within the first minute, and held Thompson against the cage. The wrestling. The grappling. Everything Wonderboy had struggled with his entire career. The crowd at MSG held its breath. Was this going to be how it ended?
But Thompson worked back to his feet. And by the end of the round, he started finding his range. The jab. The sidekick. The stance switches. Wonderboy was still in there.
[CLIP: Thompson working back to feet, finding range in Round 1]
Round two, Thompson opened up. He spent more time at distance, landing clean shots, and he appeared to stagger Radtke before the bell. The Garden started buzzing. The karate was working.
Going into the third round, the fight was tied one round apiece. And Stephen Thompson — forty-three years old, father of a five-month-old daughter, one win in his last six, fighting in the same arena where he drew with Woodley a decade ago — he knew this was it.
[FE CARD: "ROUND 3" — gold text on dark dramatic background]
Radtke was slowing down. Thompson saw it. He started letting his hands go, pushing forward, and then he threw it. The spinning wheel kick. The same technique that knocked out Jake Ellenberger eleven years earlier. The same move he'd been drilling since he was a kid at Upstate Karate.
It landed flush.

And then Stephen Thompson did what he said he wouldn't do.
He cried.

He left his gloves in the center of the octagon. The traditional retirement gesture. The crowd at Madison Square Garden — the same building that hosted his greatest fight and his most heartbreaking almost — gave him a standing ovation.
After the fight, he grabbed the microphone and said he couldn't have asked for anything better than a spinning back kick to the dome. He said he told himself he wouldn't cry. He said he had no regrets. Zero.

He walked out to Wonderboy by Tenacious D because of course he did. He never had a bad word to say about anyone. Not after the Woodley draws, not after the knockout losses, not after the losing streaks. He just smiled, went back to work, and kept being the guy everyone wished they could be.
